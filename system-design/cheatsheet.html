0. How you are actually graded

Four signals, weighted roughly equally. Most rejections are on 1 and 4, not 2.

SignalWhat “strong hire” looks like
Requirements & scopingYou narrowed an open-ended prompt to a designable problem in <8 min, stated functional + non-functional requirements, and got explicit agreement before designing
High-level designA coherent end-to-end architecture, drawn in one pass, with data flowing through it. No orphan boxes
Deep diveYou went two or three levels below the box the interviewer picked, and knew the failure modes
Tradeoff articulationFor every meaningful decision, you named the alternative and why you rejected it — unprompted

The level distinction that matters to you:

  • L4/L5 — graded on coverage. Did they remember to cache, shard, queue?
  • L6 — graded on depth + tradeoffs. Can they defend one decision under sustained pushback?
  • L7 — graded on problem framing. Do they challenge the requirement itself? Do they discuss org/ownership boundaries, migration from the existing system, and what they'd build vs. buy? An L7 who designs a beautiful greenfield system without asking “what exists today and how do we get there” has failed the round.
Silent killers: designing before scoping · never stating a number · one-way monologue · no failure discussion · defending a choice by naming a company that uses it (“Netflix does this”) instead of by reasoning · over-engineering a problem that doesn't need it.

1. The 45-minute clock

Rehearse this until it's muscle memory. It is the fix for “I discover the levers mid-flight.”

TimePhaseOutput
0–5ClarifyFunctional requirements (3–5 bullets), explicit non-goals
5–8QuantifyScale, QPS, storage, latency SLO, consistency requirement. Say the numbers out loud.
8–12API + data model3–5 endpoints, core entities, access patterns. Access patterns drive the store — always.
12–25High-level designDraw it. Client → edge → services → storage → async path → analytics
25–38Deep diveInterviewer picks, or you offer: “the two risky parts here are X and Y — which?”
38–43Failure, scale, opsWhat breaks first, how you detect it, how you degrade
43–45WrapRecap the 2–3 decisions that defined the design and what you'd revisit

Opening script (memorize the shape):

“Let me make sure I'm solving the right problem. I'll assume we're building X for Y users, and I'll treat A, B, C as in scope and D, E as out of scope. On non-functionals I'm going to prioritize [availability over consistency / read latency over write latency] — does that match what you want me to optimize? Let me put some numbers on it before I draw anything.”

Bail-out phrase when you don't know something:

“I don't have deep hands-on with [X]. What I'd reason from first principles is… and the thing I'd want to validate before committing is…”

This scores better than bluffing. Bluffing at L6+ is an automatic no-hire when caught.

Rules of thumb

  • 1 day ≈ 86,400 s ≈ 105 s. Memorize this; every QPS calc uses it.
  • 1 M writes/day ≈ 12 writes/s. 100 M/day ≈ 1,200/s. 1 B/day ≈ 12 K/s.
  • Peak = 2–5× average. Use 3× unless told otherwise.
  • 1 KB × 1 M = 1 GB. 1 KB × 1 B = 1 TB.
  • Commodity node: ~64 GB RAM, ~2 TB usable SSD, ~10 K–50 K simple QPS, ~1 Gbps NIC.
  • A single well-indexed Postgres primary: comfortably 5–10 K writes/s, tens of thousands of reads/s. Do not shard below this. Saying “we don't need to shard yet” is a senior signal.
  • Cache hit rate 80–95% is realistic. 99% is a claim you must justify.
  • Availability: 99.9% = 8.7 h/yr down. 99.99% = 52 min. 99.999% = 5 min. Each nine roughly 10× the cost.

The estimation sequence (always this order)

  1. DAU → requests/user/day → average QPS → peak QPS
  2. Payload size → bytes/s ingest → storage/year → × replication factor
  3. Read:write ratio → read QPS → cache working set (20% of a day's reads)
  4. Storage ÷ per-node capacity → shard count
  5. State which dimension dominates — that dimension is the design.

3. Concept mastery checklist

Tier 1 — do not skip. Every one of these can be a full deep dive.

Networking & protocols

  • DNS resolution, anycast, GeoDNS
  • TCP vs UDP; connection setup cost; keep-alive; head-of-line blocking
  • TLS handshake cost and termination point
  • HTTP/1.1 vs HTTP/2 (multiplexing) vs HTTP/3 (QUIC)
  • gRPC vs REST vs GraphQL — when each is right
  • Real-time: long polling vs SSE vs WebSockets — know the tradeoff table

Load balancing & routing

  • L4 vs L7; when you need L7
  • Algorithms: round robin, least connections, consistent hashing, power-of-two-choices
  • Health checks, connection draining, sticky sessions (and why to avoid them)
  • API gateway responsibilities: authn, rate limit, routing, aggregation

Caching

  • Placement: client, CDN, edge, application, database
  • Patterns: cache-aside, read-through, write-through, write-behind, refresh-ahead
  • Eviction: LRU, LFU, TTL, ARC
  • Failure modes: thundering herd / cache stampede (fix: request coalescing, jittered TTL, probabilistic early expiry), hot key (fix: key splitting, local L1), cache penetration (fix: negative caching, bloom filter), cold start after deploy
  • Invalidation strategies and why it's genuinely the hard problem

Storage internals

  • B-tree vs LSM-tree: read/write amplification, when each wins
  • Indexes: clustered vs secondary, covering index, composite index column order
  • ACID; isolation levels (read committed, repeatable read, snapshot, serializable) and the anomaly each prevents
  • MVCC, write-ahead log
  • SQL vs document vs wide-column vs key-value vs graph — chosen by access pattern, never by fashion

Replication

  • Single-leader, multi-leader, leaderless (Dynamo-style)
  • Sync vs async vs semi-sync; replication lag and read-your-writes
  • Quorum: W + R > N; sloppy quorum, hinted handoff
  • Failover, split-brain, fencing tokens
  • Conflict resolution: LWW, vector clocks, CRDTs

Partitioning / sharding

  • Range vs hash vs directory-based
  • Consistent hashing with virtual nodes — be able to draw the ring and explain rebalancing
  • Hot shard / celebrity problem
  • Resharding without downtime (double-write + backfill + cutover)
  • Cross-shard queries and why they're the tax you pay

Consistency & consensus

  • CAP — state it correctly (it's about behavior during partition, not a menu you pick from at design time). PACELC is the better framing and saying so is a senior signal.
  • The consistency ladder: linearizable → sequential → causal → read-your-writes → monotonic reads → eventual
  • Raft (leader election, log replication, commit index) at a conceptual level; Paxos by name only
  • Where consensus actually appears: leader election, config/metadata store, distributed lock (ZooKeeper, etcd, Chubby)
  • Distributed transactions: 2PC and why it blocks; Saga (orchestration vs choreography) with compensating actions; transactional outbox
  • Idempotency keys, exactly-once as an illusion (it's at-least-once + idempotent consumer)

Messaging & streaming

  • Queue vs log (SQS/RabbitMQ vs Kafka/Kinesis) — competing consumers vs replayable ordered log
  • Kafka model: topic, partition, offset, consumer group, ordering guarantee per partition only
  • Delivery semantics: at-most-once, at-least-once, effectively-once
  • Backpressure, dead letter queue, poison message, consumer lag as the key metric
  • Fan-out on write vs fan-out on read (this single tradeoff shows up in half the canonical problems)

Resilience patterns

  • Timeouts (always), retries with exponential backoff + jitter, retry budgets
  • Circuit breaker, bulkhead, load shedding, graceful degradation
  • Rate limiting: token bucket, leaky bucket, fixed window, sliding window log, sliding window counter — and how to make it work distributed
  • Idempotency, at-least-once + dedupe
  • Failure domains, blast radius, cell-based architecture

Tier 2 — know well enough to deep-dive on one

  • Search: inverted index, tokenization, TF-IDF/BM25, Elasticsearch sharding, near-real-time indexing
  • Geospatial: geohash, quadtree, S2 cells, R-tree — plus the “find drivers near me” pattern
  • Probabilistic structures: Bloom filter, HyperLogLog, Count-Min Sketch, Top-K. Know when each buys you memory
  • Object storage + CDN: presigned URLs, multipart upload, chunking, cache-control, edge invalidation
  • Time series: downsampling, rollups, retention tiers, columnar storage
  • Data engineering: CDC, batch vs stream, event-time vs processing-time, watermarks, lambda vs kappa
  • Observability: RED/USE metrics, distributed tracing, structured logs, SLI/SLO/error budget
  • Security: OAuth2/OIDC, JWT vs session, encryption in transit and at rest, secrets management, least privilege

Tier 3 — bonus, only if it's directly relevant

  • Vector databases, ANN indexes (HNSW, IVF), embedding pipelines, RAG serving — treat this as Tier 1 if your background invites ML-infra-flavored prompts
  • Feature stores, online/offline skew
  • Multi-tenancy isolation models
  • Cell-based / shuffle sharding

6. The phased schedule

Phases 1–2 — Primitives (no mock problems yet).
Read Designing Data-Intensive Applications cover to cover — Ch. 5–9 twice. Add the DDIA-adjacent papers: Dynamo, Bigtable, GFS, Kafka, Spanner, MapReduce. Goal: you can explain replication, partitioning, and consistency without reaching for an example system.

Phase 3 — Framework + estimation.
Drill the 45-min structure. 20 timed estimation reps. Build the muscle of stating a number before drawing a box.

Phase 4 — Canonical problems.
5 per week. Write your own answer cold on paper first (45 min, timed), then compare against a reference. The writing-cold step is non-negotiable — reading solutions creates false confidence.

Phase 5 — Mocks. Minimum 12, with real engineers.
This is the phase that decides the outcome and the one people skip. Record them. Watch yourself. You are looking for: did I scope before designing, did I state numbers, did I name alternatives unprompted, did I talk more than 70% of the time (bad).

Phase 6 — Weak spots + the rest of the loop.
Deep dives on your two weakest Tier-1 areas. Plus: coding still exists at L6 (LeetCode medium, ~150 problems, patterns not volume), and at L7 the behavioral/leadership rounds carry more weight than system design.

Weekly cadence throughout: 6–8 focused hours. Sunday review of every mistake from that week. A one-page written summary per problem you solve — writing is what makes it stick.

  • System Design Interview Vol. 1 & 2, Alex Xu — problem coverage and structure
  • Educative: Grokking the Modern System Design Interview
  • ByteByteGo (Xu's newsletter/videos) — good for pattern recall

Papers (skim for the idea, don't grind)
Dynamo · Bigtable · GFS · MapReduce · Spanner · Kafka · Raft (extended) · Chubby · Borg · Cassandra

Engineering blogs — Netflix, Uber, Airbnb, Stripe, Discord, Cloudflare, Meta. Read for how they explain tradeoffs, which is the exact skill being graded.

Mocks — interviewing.io (real FAANG interviewers, best signal), Pramp, or a peer group. Budget for this; it's the highest-ROI spend.

8. Pre-interview one-pager

Read this 20 minutes before the round.

  1. Scope before you design. Get explicit agreement.
  2. Say a number in the first 8 minutes.
  3. Draw the happy path end to end before you optimize anything.
  4. For every choice, name the alternative and why you rejected it — before being asked.
  5. Offer the deep dive: “the two risky parts are X and Y — which do you want?”
  6. Discuss what breaks first and how you'd detect it.
  7. Say “I don't know” when you don't. Then reason from first principles.
  8. Stop talking. Check in every few minutes. It's a collaboration, not a lecture.
  9. Don't over-engineer. “At this scale a single primary is fine” is a strong-hire sentence.
  10. Close by recapping the 2–3 decisions that defined the design.
